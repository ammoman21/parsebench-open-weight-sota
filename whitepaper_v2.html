for Insurance Document Parsing

Abstract

Automated insurance underwriting requires parsing large volumes of visually complex documents—rate filings, loss runs, policy forms—into structured, machine-readable text. On ParseBench, a public document-parsing benchmark whose Tables dimension is 57.7% insurance documents, the leading open-weight parser scores 3.73 points lower on insurance tables than on the non-insurance remainder, indicating that this document class is measurably harder than the corpus average for existing systems. We study whether a small open-weight model can be improved on this benchmark through failure-driven fine-tuning. Starting from KDL-Frontier-Parser-nano (1.2B parameters), we first reproduce the published baseline in our own environment, diagnose its dominant failure mode (the model emits no inline formatting markup at all, and prompting cannot fix this), construct 6,678 training pairs from SEC EDGAR insurance-carrier filings and synthetically rendered pages, and train a low-rank adapter on the language layers only, leaving the production prompt byte-identical. The resulting pipeline, florin-parser-nano, entered the leaderboard at 76.69 Overall (mean of two full-corpus runs, 76.72 / 76.66)—at its merge on August 28, 2026, the highest score of any vision–language model on the board, open-weight or proprietary, and fifth overall behind four commercial entries. The same-environment improvement over our reproduction of the previous open-weight leader is +4.04 points. A v3 revision (September 2026) adds a second, table-structure adapter—rank 16, language layers only, trained one epoch on 3,000 synthetic table renders whose hierarchical headers and merged cells mirror the rate tables, filed through SERFF (the U.S. state rate-and-form filing system), that dominate the corpus—and one emission rule (whole-line bold), raising the entry to 77.50 (mean of four full-corpus runs, 77.465 / 77.572 / 77.472 / 77.474, spread 0.107), with the per-dimension trade disclosed: Charts −1.9, Semantic Formatting +5.6. Alongside the entry we disclose six benchmark defects (filed upstream as issues #100–#104 and #131), and we document a maintainer-review correction in which two defects in our own emission code were found, fixed, and re-scored on identical model output the same day, revising our score from 76.92 to 76.69. All claims were preregistered before measurement; this is a benchmark study, and the model is not deployed in any production system. Following the September 1 addition of Anthropic Fable 5.1 (78.92, proprietary), the v3 entry—pending maintainer re-verification—is the top open-weight vision–language model and second among all VLMs (see Addendum).

1  Introduction

Florin is building a full-stack insurance company that operates with no human underwriters; central to that thesis is software capable of autonomous underwriting. A prerequisite for any such system is document parsing at scale: underwriting decisions consume rate filings submitted through SERFF (the System for Electronic Rate and Form Filing, the channel through which United States insurance carriers file rates with state regulators), loss-run reports, policy forms, actuarial memoranda, and insurer financial statements. These documents are dominated by dense, deeply nested tables—rate relativity grids, per-policy rate tables, merged cells spanning rating dimensions—and by meaning-bearing typography: struck-through text marks superseded terms, and superscripts are footnote references that change how a figure reads. A parser that silently drops them returns text that is complete and misleading.

Public evidence suggests existing systems handle this document class worse than average enterprise documents. ParseBench[1], a live document-parsing benchmark maintained by LlamaIndex, draws 57.7% of its Tables dimension (290 of 503 pages) from insurance documents, and our measurement of the benchmark's leading open-weight parser shows it scoring 84.18 on those insurance tables versus 87.91 on the non-insurance remainder—a 3.73-point gap on the largest, most reliable subset in the corpus (Section 2.2). The published Tables scores on this benchmark are therefore being carried upward by the non-insurance minority, not the reverse.

Motivated by this gap, we investigate how far a small open-weight model can be pushed on this benchmark by a disciplined loop of baseline reproduction, failure diagnosis, targeted data construction, and parameter-efficient fine-tuning. Our contributions are:

  • A fine-tuned 1.2B-parameter open-weight parser, florin-parser-nano, whose version of record scored 76.69 Overall on ParseBench—at its August 28, 2026 merge, the top score in the benchmark's vision–language-model (VLM) category, ahead of both the then-best proprietary VLM (Gemini 3 Flash, 75.05) and the previous open-weight leader (76.36)—updated in v3 to 77.50 over four full-corpus runs (Sections 3.7 and 4.1), with weights, training code, and data generators public.
  • A same-environment, preregistered evaluation protocol in which every improvement is measured against our own reproduction of the baseline (72.65), not against published numbers we could not reproduce.
  • Six benchmark and scorer defects disclosed to the maintainers (issues #100–#104 and #131[7]), plus a documented correction episode in which maintainer review found two defects in our own emission code; both were fixed and re-scored on identical model output the same day (Section 5).

Consistent with the public preregistration[5], this work is a benchmark study conducted for publication. The model was not built for, and is not deployed in, a production system.

2  Background: ParseBench

2.1  What the benchmark measures

ParseBench scores document parsers over 2,079 annotated pages of enterprise documents using 169,011 deterministic rule checks. A parser converts each page image (or PDF) to markdown; the markdown is graded against per-page annotations across five dimensions, and the Overall score is the unweighted mean of the five. Because grading is rule-based (with one optional LLM-judge component discussed in Section 5), runs are reproducible and inexpensive. Submission is self-serve: a pull request adds a results file and provider code to the benchmark repository, and maintainers review and re-run entries. At this entry's merge (August 28, 2026) the leaderboard held 88 entries: 57 vision–language models (31 open-weight, 26 proprietary), 22 commercial parsing products, and 9 local open-source tools; at the v3 revision (September 4, 2026, board state at harness commit 99bb1d16) it holds 94: 62 VLMs (34 open-weight, 28 proprietary), 23 commercial products, and 9 local tools.

2.2  Corpus composition: an insurance-heavy benchmark

Three independent passes over the corpus—filenames, all 169,011 ground-truth rule strings, and the text layer of the PDFs—identify 384 of 2,079 documents (18.5%) as insurance, with the concentration in exactly the dimension that matters most for document extraction: the Tables dimension is majority insurance (193 SERFF rate-filing pages, 78 further pages from auto and farm property–casualty filings, plus Solvency II reports and life-insurer financial statements). On our full-corpus baseline run, the leading open-weight parser scored 84.18 on the 290 insurance table pages versus 87.91 on the 213 non-insurance table pages (−3.73). This is the one insurance-subset comparison large enough to support a confident claim; the other four dimensions' insurance slices are 24–40 pages and we treat them as directional only.

3  Method

Both fine-tuning campaigns described below—the version-of-record formatting adapter (Sections 3.2–3.5) and the v3 table-structure adapter (Section 3.7)—are failure-driven: each targets a failure mode measured on the benchmark corpus, not guessed. One consequence follows from the corpus composition of Section 2.2 and is worth stating for the insurance reader: because the Tables dimension of that corpus is 57.7% insurance filings, failure modes measured on tables are predominantly failure modes on insurance documents, and a lever built against them is disproportionately an insurance-document lever. The training data itself, described in Sections 3.3 and 3.7, does not consist of insurance intake documents and was selected for typography and table structure, not insurance subject matter; the connection to insurance runs through what the failures were measured on and what document shapes the generators reproduce, and no further.

3.1  Baseline reproduction and parity discipline

The base model is KDLAI/KDL-Frontier-Parser-nano[3] (KoreaDeepLearning; 1.2B parameters, Qwen2-VL architecture, AGPL-3.0), which held the top open-weight position with a published Overall of 76.36. Before changing anything we reproduced its pipeline in our own environment: vLLM serving on a single NVIDIA H100 80GB with the pipeline's own documented flags, the complete 2,079-file corpus, the benchmark's own evaluation code and default metric per dimension, and the chart LLM normalizer disabled and declared (LLAMACLOUD_BENCH_LLM_NORMALIZATION=off; see Section 5).

The consequence for claims is fixed in the preregistration: every improvement is measured against our own 72.65 reproduction in the same environment; the comparison to the published 76.36 crosses evaluation environments and is always reported with that caveat.

3.2  Failure diagnosis

Across 23,802 recognized elements from 1,196 documents, the base pipeline emits zero inline formatting markup—no bold, strikethrough, superscript, or subscript markers of any kind. Its instruction set is five bare prompt strings (e.g. "\nText Recognition:\n") with no formatting request anywhere, so 100% of its Semantic Formatting bold credit arrives through an indirect scorer path (headings render bold; see Section 5). Of the scored bold failures, 63.3% are text merged inline into a longer output line: the characters are present but not separable, so no post-processing can reach them—only the model marking spans at generation time can.

Prompting cannot supply this behavior. Four prompt variants were measured on a 40-document development subset against the byte-exact production prompt as control: the model can emit markers when asked (1,729 markers under the most explicit variant, versus zero under control), but it marks the wrong spans, and its bold sub-score degrades monotonically with the amount of prompt perturbation (60.59 → 58.96 → 56.94 → 55.24). All four variants scored at or below control Overall. Two design decisions follow directly: the thing to train is span-level judgment, not marker syntax; and training must leave the production prompt byte-identical, because the model is brittle to any prompt change.

3.3  Training data: EDGAR fragments and synthetic rendering

We built 6,678 region-crop→markdown training pairs from two sources, neither of which touches ParseBench's annotations (which are the test set; using them would be contamination):

  • 2,165 real fragments from SEC EDGAR (the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission's public filing system[8]). The harvester selects insurance-sector issuers by the SEC's own industry coding (SIC codes 6311/6321/6331/6411—life, accident and health, property–casualty, and insurance brokerage; carriers such as Travelers, Allstate, Progressive, Chubb, and AIG) and takes their most recent annual and quarterly reports (10-K/10-Q). Because EDGAR filings exist as HTML, bold ground truth is derived mechanically from the document object model rather than annotated by hand. The ceiling of this connection is stated plainly: these are insurance carriers' financial filings, real-document typography with exact labels—not insurance intake documents (applications, loss runs, ACORD forms)—and the fragments were selected for styling coverage, not insurance content.
  • 3,313 synthetic rendered fragments: business-document pages generated in HTML/CSS with known bold runs, strikethrough amendments, and superscript/subscript footnote references, rendered to images at pipeline resolution—so the target markdown is exact by construction. This is the sole source of strikethrough and super/subscript supervision, and includes CJK newsprint and Devanagari textures to match the corpus's multilingual pages.
  • 1,200 no-styling negatives whose correct output contains no markers, to teach restraint: the measured failure mode of explicit prompting was indiscriminate marking, and without negatives a fine-tune reproduces it.

3.4  Fine-tuning configuration

We trained a LoRA adapter (low-rank adaptation[9]: the base weights stay frozen and small low-rank correction matrices are trained) with rank 16 on the language model's attention and MLP layers only; the vision tower and the vision–language projector are frozen. The loss is completion-only (computed on the target markdown, not the prompt), and the prompt is byte-identical to the production pipeline's "\nText Recognition:\n". Training ran 2 epochs at learning rate 5e-5 in bfloat16 on a single H100, roughly 50 minutes per run. The full campaign was 7 training iterations selected on the 40-document development subset (never on the benchmark's published leaderboard), about 9 hours and $31 of GPU time end to end, followed by the two preregistered full-corpus runs.

3.5  Emission layer

Every VLM entry on ParseBench is scored on markdown produced jointly by the model and declared provider code in the benchmark repository—the code that converts the model's per-element output (category, bounding box, text) into the final markdown string. Our entry is no exception, and we state its emission layer plainly: the scored output is the fine-tuned model plus the provider florin_parser_nano.py submitted in PR #99[6], which carries three emission changes relative to the base pipeline's provider, each preregistered as a defect fix or a real inference the pipeline was not making:

  • heading levels derived from title-element bounding-box height (the base pipeline performs no hierarchy inference; every heading collapses to a single level);
  • bold applied to run-in Label: prefixes, which are bold in the source documents and dropped by the base pipeline;
  • a relaxed heading gate that stops rejecting genuine headings ending in a colon, with a preregistered 20-word length cap.

A fourth preregistered fix (a missing section_header key in the base pipeline's category map, dead code confirmed by zero occurrences across 2,078 artifacts) ships in the provider but is excluded from all arithmetic, because replay cannot measure its contribution. The emission patches without any fine-tuning were measured by replay at 74.64 Overall (+1.99 over the 72.65 reproduction; a pre-correction replay figure, re-measurement pending)—short of the published leader, which is why the fine-tune, not the emission layer, is the substance of this entry. The final emission code is exactly the version corrected after maintainer review (Section 5.2).

3.6  Preregistration

Before any measured run we committed a public preregistration[5] fixing the base model, serving flags, corpus, metrics, run protocol (one validation run, one frozen confirmation run, tolerance 0.3 Overall between them), kill criteria (any change costing more than 0.3 on Tables, Content Faithfulness, or Visual Grounding is dropped regardless of gains elsewhere), and three claim tiers tied to score thresholds. One dated amendment corrected a mischaracterization in the original document before any live run and re-measured the affected figures; the amendment is preserved verbatim alongside the original text.

3.7  The v3 update: a table-structure adapter and one emission rule

A September 2026 revision (v3; submitted as PR #142, updating the row merged from PR #99) adds two components to the version-of-record pipeline, both disclosed in full in the pull request. First, a second LoRA adapter—rank 16, alpha 32, on the language model's attention and MLP layers only, vision tower frozen, matching the version-of-record recipe—trained for one epoch at learning rate 2e-5 on 3,000 synthetic HTML-rendered table crops with exact OTSL targets. (OTSL is the pipeline's table token language: cell, span, and row-break tokens that deterministically encode a table grid.) Every emitted target is round-tripped through the pipeline's own OTSL parser and asserted cell-for-cell equal to the grid the renderer was given, so targets cannot drift from their images; no benchmark data is read or used. The recipe follows the one CloudRaker published alongside rakedoc-nano (see Addendum), with our own generator. Second, one added emission rule: a standalone plain-text line of at most 14 words—blank lines or document edges on both sides, outside tables, code fences, lists, and existing markup—is wrapped in bold, because short standalone lines in business documents are headings, captions, or labels, set bold in print.

The generator's structural profile is matched to the corpus it targets: median four columns, two-tier hierarchical headers, row- and column-spanning (merged) cells, stub heads, and spanned total rows—the structural profile of the SERFF rate tables that make the corpus's Tables dimension 57.7% insurance (Section 2.2)—with label vocabulary drawn from property–casualty reporting (lines of business, accident-year and calendar-year groupings, loss and combined ratios). Per Section 3's ceiling statement, the data is synthetic throughout: no real insurance document appears in it.

Measured effects, stated plainly. The emission rule was measured by replay on stored artifacts before any live run: Content Faithfulness delta +0.0004 (the scorer's text normalization strips bold markers), table markup byte-identical on all 503 table documents, Visual Grounding invariant by construction. On the full corpus the v3 composite trades Charts down for Semantic Formatting up—Charts 65.19 → 63.28 (−1.9), Semantic Formatting 70.64 → 76.27 (+5.6), with Tables, Content Faithfulness, and Visual Grounding approximately unchanged—and we disclose the mechanism we found: the adapter, though trained on table structure, generalized whole-line-bold behavior (visible in raw model output with no emission layer), and the benchmark's styling matcher credits substring queries against bolded lines while its title-hierarchy metric accepts a whole-line-bold line as a heading event, so the behavior earns more than the whole-line annotation count alone suggests. Anyone weighting Charts specifically should prefer the version-of-record weights, which remain available in the model repository's history. Three sibling levers from the same campaign failed their preregistered gates and are reported as negative results in the pull request: a span-styling adapter killed for Charts collateral (+2.24 Semantic Formatting, −2.38 Charts in a matched split evaluation), and two chart-to-table adapters that scored perfectly on held-out synthetic charts while regressing real-benchmark Charts by 13.9 and 17.3 points.

5  Findings: benchmark defects and the correction episode

5.1  Six defects disclosed upstream (issues #100–#104 and #131)

Working at parity depth surfaced defects in the benchmark itself, filed as GitHub issues against the benchmark repository[7]:

  • #100 — Chart rule rejects correct extraction on typographic grounds. The chart data-point scorer only credits a chart-title label found in surrounding text if that text is bold, a heading, or a caption; a parser that extracts every data point correctly but renders the title as plain text loses the points.
  • #101 — Zero-weight annotation categories, undocumented. is_italic (655 rules), is_underline (405), and is_mark (88) are annotated in the dataset and evaluated, but contribute to no scored category—the styling sub-score is built from bold, strikethrough, superscript, and subscript only.
  • #102 — Silent default layout adapter. For a provider key with no registered layout adapter, the registry silently returns a default adapter instead of raising, which makes the intended shape-matching fallback unreachable; a new pipeline can score approximately zero on Visual Grounding with no registry-level error anywhere.
  • #103 — LLM-judge normalization on by default, contradicting the documentation. Chart evaluation applies a Claude-Haiku LLM normalization pass unless an environment variable is explicitly set to off; the README describes evaluation as deterministic and two docstrings state the default is off. (All runs in this paper declare it off.)
  • #104 — Bold–heading coupling. The bold matcher accepts any heading line containing the annotated text as evidence of bold. Because the base pipeline emits no inline markup, 100% of its bold credit arrives through this route, and the dataset contains zero negative styling rules, so indiscriminate promotion costs nothing inside the dimension. By replay, a markdown-only transformation set containing no bold detection at all measured 75.65 Overall—within 0.71 points of the then-leading published score. We disclosed this instead of submitting it; the measurement and reasoning are in the preregistration's amendment.
  • #131 — Visual Grounding: layout documents with order rules silently drop all their element rules. When a layout document also carries reading-order rules, the loader constructs it as a plain parse test case and silently discards its layout element rules: 64 of 500 layout documents and 1,472 of 15,688 element rules never enter Visual Grounding scoring at all, while every affected document still reports success. Visual Grounding scores across the board are therefore computed on a silently reduced rule set, and cross-environment comparisons of that dimension (including our own unresolved 4.65-point reproduction gap, Section 3.1) become harder to interpret when the denominator varies with loader behavior. Filed September 2, 2026, from the v3 campaign's audit of that gap—the same audit trail as issues #100–#104.

5.2  Maintainer review found two defects in our emission code—the correction

We report this episode in full because it is evidence the process works. Our entry was submitted at Overall 76.92 (run means 76.95 / 76.89). The ParseBench maintainer's review of PR #99 found two defects in our markdown emission that inflated Semantic Formatting: (1) the run-in-label bolder matched against the whole line, so a list item - Note: x became **- Note:** x—the bullet swallowed into the bold span, the list broken—and, lacking fence-state tracking, it bolded label-shaped lines inside code/LaTeX fences; because the scorer's bold matcher searches raw text, the corrupted spans still earned credit; (2) our relaxed heading gate had dropped the stock terminal-punctuation veto entirely, promoting short sentences ending in a period to headings.

Both defects were reproduced byte-for-byte against the submitted code, fixed, and covered with 16 new tests (including scorer-acceptance checks against the benchmark's real rule classes) the same day. Both submitted runs were then replayed through the fixed emission on identical stored model output—no new inference, so any score change is caused by the emission code alone. The replay's validity was checked directly: re-scoring the stored markdown reproduced the stored per-document metrics with zero mismatches on every rule-scored split of both runs, and the fixed provider port was verified byte-identical to the fixed emission on all 4,156 documents.

6  Limitations

  • Few runs, no confidence intervals. The version of record is the mean of N=2 full-corpus runs (spread 0.06 Overall); the v3 update is the mean of N=4 (spread 0.107). The agreement is tight, but no formal uncertainty estimate accompanies either, and the margins over neighboring entries are small (0.33 over the base model's published row for the version of record; 0.27 over rakedoc-nano, the nearest open-weight entry, for v3).
  • The v3 gain concentrates where the scorer is most permissive. Section 3.7 disclosed that whole-line bold earns credit through two scorer paths beyond literal bold annotations—a coupling in the same family as issue #104. The v3 Semantic Formatting gain is therefore partly a property of the scorer, not only of the parse, and it is bought with a real Charts regression (−1.9); both facts are stated in PR #142 rather than left for reviewers to find.
  • The emission layer's contribution is not isolated from the weights. The +4.04 same-environment gain combines the fine-tuned weights and the three emission patches. A clean decomposition—base-model output through our emission, and our model's output through the baseline emission—can be computed entirely by replay on stored outputs at zero GPU cost; it is pending and will be published in the reproduction repository. The patch-only replay figure of 74.64 predates the Section 5.2 correction and is likewise pending re-measurement.
  • The insurance-subset score of our model is pending re-measurement, and remains absent in v3. Our model's insurance-subset figure was measured before the emission correction, so we do not report it here; the subset scripts re-run mechanically once the corrected reports are in place, and the v3 runs have not yet been scored on the subset either. The baseline's insurance-vs-other gap (84.18 vs 87.91, Section 2.2) is unaffected, as both sides of that comparison come from the same baseline run.
  • The cross-environment comparison is unresolved. We measure the base pipeline at 72.65 where its published row says 76.36, and could not explain the difference (Section 3.1). If the maintainers' environment re-runs our fixed pipeline and lands below ours, their numbers govern—the pull request states this explicitly.
  • Benchmark-specific supervision. The training data targets exactly the styling behaviors ParseBench scores (bold, strikethrough, super/subscript). We built it from real filings and exact-by-construction renders rather than from benchmark annotations, but transfer beyond this benchmark's rule set is unmeasured.
  • Not a production system. Per the preregistration, this work is for publication only. No claim is made that this model is used in, or was built for, a deployed underwriting pipeline.

7  Reproducibility

Everything required to re-derive the numbers in this paper is public:

  • Reproduction repository[5]: training loop, data generators, EDGAR harvester, evaluation harness, replay scripts, per-iteration logs, the preregistration with its dated amendments, and the defect-response report for the Section 5.2 correction.
  • Model weights[4]: LoRA adapters and merged weights at florin-inc/florin-parser-nano, AGPL-3.0 (inherited from the base model), with full attribution to KoreaDeepLearning, the results file, and the model card carrying the dated correction note. The v3 composite is bit-reproducible from the version-of-record weights plus the 34 MB table-structure adapter via the published merge script, and verifiable by checksum (sha256 7214d61d…, stated in full in PR #142).
  • Benchmark entry[6]: PR #99 to the benchmark repository contains the provider code (the declared emission layer), the leaderboard row, and the review thread of the correction episode; PR #142 contains the v3 update (adapter lineage, all four per-run tables, the Charts trade and its mechanism, and the negative results); issues #100–#104 and #131 contain the defect reports with file and line references.
  • Instrument integrity: no file under the upstream benchmark checkout was modified; all changes live in our own provider module. The replay harness reproduces the baseline's stored scores exactly (zero per-document mismatches on every rule-scored split), which is what makes the correction episode's emission-only attribution possible.
