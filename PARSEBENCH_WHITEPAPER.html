ParseBench: path to a published state-of-the-art result

A live document-parsing benchmark whose table corpus is majority insurance rate filings. We can take the top open-weight position on it without training anything. The open question is not whether we can — it is which version of the claim survives a technical reader.

Where we stand

Measured, full 476-document scoring corpus — as of 12 Aug; superseded, see Outcome above 76.79  vs  76.36

Our patched pipeline against the published score of the open-weight leader, KDL-Frontier-Parser-nano (KoreaDeepLearning; 1.2B parameters, AGPL-3.0). Margin +0.43 against an estimated ±0.2 transfer uncertainty. Achieved by changing markdown emission only — no fine-tuning, no GPU training.

Superseded 17 Aug: this 76.79 included a patch member later reclassified as exploitation; the defensible set re-measured at 74.64 (preregistration Amendment 1). The campaign closed at 76.92 via the fine-tune — see the Outcome box above.

Five things are simultaneously true, and the tension between them is the decision:

  • The measurement rig is validated. Three of five dimensions reproduce the leader's published scores within 0.3, and a control model reproduces its published chart score exactly.
  • The gap was real and is now closed. Our unpatched reproduction scored 72.65; the patch set recovers 4.14 points.
  • Part of that recovery is genuine parsing improvement; part is exploiting a coupling in the scorer. A 20-line markdown transformation alone reaches 77.01.
  • Four defects found — one in the leader's pipeline, two in the benchmark's scoring, one in a different benchmark. Contributions independent of any score.
  • The corpus is on-thesis: 57.7% of the Tables dimension is insurance rate filings, and the leader performs worse on those than on the remainder.
01

The target, and why this one

ParseBench (LlamaIndex) scores document parsing over ~2,079 human-verified pages of enterprise documents using 169,011 deterministic rule checks across five dimensions. Output is markdown; grading is rule-based, so runs are reproducible and cheap.

It was selected after rejecting five alternatives for verified reasons, not preference: INS-ActBench (actuarial) publishes evaluation code but no dataset — 3 commits, 0 releases, and a HuggingFace request to host the data unanswered for two weeks; InsureBench never launched and its leaderboard chart is placeholder art; SnorkelUnderwrite is archived with all 33 entries self-run; ActuBench is saturated at 0.98 with no submission endpoint; and the Berkeley Function-Calling Leaderboard is frozen at December 2025, so its #1 is a nine-month-old model.

ParseBench clears three bars none of those cleared together:

  • Live and current — 85 entries, 39 open-weight.
  • Self-serve submission — a pull request adding .eval_results/parsebench.yaml to your own model repo. No form, no email, no gatekeeper.
  • No LLM-as-judge on the dimensions we target, so a full run costs GPU time only and the score cannot drift between attempts.

Why formatting and grounding are the load-bearing dimensions for us

Both encode requirements a real document pipeline has. Struck-through text marks superseded terms; superscripts are footnote references that change how a figure reads — a parser that silently drops them returns text that is complete and misleading. Grounding is auditability: an extracted figure you cannot trace to a page region is unusable where decisions must be defensible.

02

Why it is effectively an insurance benchmark

Three independent passes over the corpus — filenames, all 169,011 ground-truth rule strings, and the text layer of 2,037 PDFs — with every candidate inspected individually and 11 judgement calls documented. 384 of 2,079 documents (18.5%) are insurance. The concentration is the point:

The Tables dimension is majority insurance: 193 SERFF rate-filing pages (System for Electronic Rate and Form Filing — the channel US carriers use to file rates with state regulators), 78 more from auto and farm property-casualty filings, plus Solvency II reports and life-insurer financial statements.

And the leader scores 3.73 points worse on insurance tables (84.18) than on the remainder (87.91) — these documents are denser and more deeply nested than the average enterprise page. So insurance table extraction is simultaneously the largest lever on that dimension and the work closest to the product.

Reference points on the same board: Anthropic Fable 5 at 70.10 and $15.60/page; LlamaParse Agentic (the benchmark authors' own commercial product) leads overall at 84.88 and $1.25/page. A self-hostable open-weight winner carries a cost argument alongside accuracy.

A 14-point shortfall has two possible causes with opposite remedies: our configuration is wrong, or the leader's published run used an environment we cannot reproduce. Distinguishing them needed a control.

Control: a second pipeline with known published scores

We served Chandra-ocr-2 and ran the same corpus. It reproduced its published Charts score exactly (65.10 vs 65.10) and its Semantic Formatting to within 3.17 — against the leader's 14.39. A metric-mapping or configuration error on our side would have hit both models similarly. It did not. Scoring is sound; the deficits are per-model.

The two models also fail in opposite dimensions — the leader is weak on formatting and fine on grounding; Chandra is 19 points low on grounding and nearly correct on formatting. That asymmetry points at per-model serving configuration rather than a harness fault. Two candidate causes were tested and eliminated: an optional LLM normaliser (scoped to chart metrics only, and our charts matched) and output truncation (zero finish_reason: "length" across 1,200 saved outputs).

04

Root cause of the formatting deficit

Across 23,802 elements from 1,196 documents, the leader emits zero inline markup — no <b>, ~~, <sup>, <sub>, <u>. Element content is captured after the model call and before markdown assembly, so this measures the model's output, not the renderer.

The cause is the prompts. The complete instruction set is five bare strings at kdl_frontier_nano.py:2596-2604 — "\nText Recognition:\n", "\nTable Recognition:\n", and three siblings. No system prompt, no schema, no formatting request anywhere in 3,323 lines. The model is never asked for formatting, so it doesn't produce any. This is a prompt gap, not a post-processing gap and not a capability gap.

The coupling that matters

The scorer's bold check accepts two kinds of evidence: an explicit bold marker, or the annotated text appearing on a #-heading line — a reasonable proxy, since headings render bold.

Since this pipeline emits no bold markers, 100% of its bold credit arrives through the heading route. Its Semantic Formatting score is, in effect, a measurement of how many lines it labels as headings. That coupling is what makes the dimension both fixable and gameable, and it is the crux of §06.

Failure-mode breakdown of the 1,303 is_bold failures: 63.3% are text merged inline into a longer line (unreachable by any renderer — only a prompt change reaches them), 18.4% are text missing entirely, and only 13.3% are heading-gate rejections. The gates that matter are a 12-word cap and a terminal-punctuation veto; the leading-capital gate we originally suspected accounts for 3 of 1,303.

05

Four defects found

Defect 1 — the leader's pipeline

Section-header is never emitted — 0 occurrences across all 2,078 artifacts — because NATIVE_LAYOUT_CATEGORY_MAP:545-572 has no section_header key. Every heading collapses to h1, making 12.9% of title_hierarchy_percent structurally unreachable for this pipeline. A missing dictionary key capping part of its own score.

Defect 2 — the benchmark's chart rule

ChartDataPointRule:370-394 only accepts a chart title label when it is bold or heading text. A parser that renders chart titles as plain text loses those points regardless of extraction accuracy. This is why our bold patches raise Charts by +2.13 without touching chart extraction — a real defect we were losing points to.

Defect 3 — undocumented zero-weight categories

is_underline, is_italic and is_mark are annotated in the dataset but belong to no scored category — evaluators/parse.py:344-357 builds the styling sub-score from only is_bold, is_strikeout, is_sup, is_sub. Confirmed in our own output: a page with two underline rules and one strikeout rule reports included_types: ["is_strikeout"]. Not discoverable from documentation; we nearly spent a day on underline.

Defect 4 — a different benchmark, and the most consequential

On the Berkeley Function-Calling Leaderboard, requests that timed out and never reached the model had the error string stored as the model's answer, which the scorer then tried to parse as a function call and marked wrong. One model's published run lost 240 of 4,641 items (5.2%) this way — 50% of one category. Four of the top 30 models exceed 2%. Corrected, that model's multi-turn average moves from 34.75 to 41.68 and one category shifts 15.8 points. Published rankings there partly measure network reliability.

The decision, today

Because the pipeline emits no real markup, promoting lines to headings raises the bold score without detecting bold. Any reviewer who reads the diff will see it. Two coherent positions:

(a) Rank-first. Submit Set A, land ~76.79, hold top open-weight on a +0.43 margin against ±0.2 uncertainty. Defensible on the letter — deterministic scoring, disclosed patch — but thin, and exposed to a maintainer re-run in an environment that reproduces the base at 76.36 where we get 72.65.

(b) Credibility-first. Submit the genuine patches only, report the number honestly even if it lands under 76.36, and publish the coupling as a finding: a 20-line markdown transformation exceeds the state of the art without improving parsing, because the bold matcher credits headings and this pipeline emits no markup. With the four defects, that is five contributions to a live benchmark.

Recommended: (b), with (a)'s number disclosed in the same document. A re-run can take a rank away; it cannot take a finding away. And it is the same species of result as Defect 4, which is already the most defensible thing this effort has produced.

07

Remaining upside

Semantic Formatting cannot carry the result alone. At the published 66.81 exactly we would score 75.53 — still 0.83 short — because the 4.65-point Visual Grounding deficit is untouched. Set A clears 76.36 only because it also gains 2.13 on Charts via Defect 2.

Grounding is the better remaining target for two reasons. It is worth more than any formatting patch still available. And Defect 1 plausibly contributes to it — a pipeline that never emits Section-header is misclassifying elements, and classification is a scored component of layout_element_rule_pass_rate. One fix may move both dimensions. It is also harder: grounding depends on localization accuracy, not output formatting, so it cannot be reached by markdown changes.

Field context worth having: open-weight models have already reached parity with the best commercial systems on Content Faithfulness (gap 0.22), Visual Grounding (0.01) and Tables (2.30). The entire open-versus-closed gap on this benchmark is Charts (25.72) and Semantic Formatting (15.94) — which is why those are the only two dimensions worth touching, and why changes elsewhere risk regression for no upside.
