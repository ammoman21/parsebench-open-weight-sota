Reproducing the ParseBench open-weight leader

Three defects in the scorer, one bug in the leading pipeline, and a corpus that is majority insurance regulatory filings

Abstract

We attempted to reproduce the leading open-weight entry on ParseBench, a document-parsing benchmark, and could not. We measure 72.65 against its published 76.36, with three of five dimensions matching within 0.3 points and two missing badly. A control experiment on a second, unrelated pipeline reproduced its published chart score exactly, establishing that the scoring is sound and the deficits are specific to the pipeline under test rather than to our setup.

Investigating the two deficits surfaced four defects. In the benchmark: the chart rule silently rejects correct data extraction when the chart title is not rendered bold; three annotated style categories — underline, italic and highlight — contribute to no score at all, which is undocumented; and the bold check credits heading markup, which for a pipeline emitting no inline markup makes the formatting dimension movable by reformatting alone. In the leading pipeline: no raw layout label it emits reaches the category carrying 18.4% of all visual-grounding rules, and the code branch that would render that category is unreachable.

We also characterise the corpus: 57.7% of the Tables dimension is insurance rate filings, and the leading pipeline scores 3.73 points worse on them than on the remainder. Repairing only what is defensible as a defect fix raises our reproduction by 1.99 points to 74.64; we make no ranking claim. All measurement code, saved measurement records and pinned upstream commits are released.

1Contributions

The defects have two different owners, which matters for who should fix them.

Three are in the benchmark's scoring:

  1. A chart rule requiring bold titles, penalising correct data extraction on typographic grounds (§3).
  2. Three annotated categories with zero weight — graded in the dataset, scored in no category, not documented (§4).
  3. A bold/heading coupling that makes the formatting dimension movable without improving parsing. We demonstrate it and decline to use it (§5).

One is in the leading pipeline:

  1. An unreachable category: no raw layout label the pipeline emits maps to Section-header, which carries 18.4% of grounding rules, and the branch rendering it is dead code (§3).

Plus: a corpus characterisation showing the Tables dimension is majority insurance regulatory filings (§6), and our own results with their limits (§8–9).

2Method

ParseBench grades deterministically by fixed rules, so a run is reproducible and a discrepancy is meaningful rather than sampling noise. We ran the benchmark's own evaluation code, at a pinned commit, using its own default metric per dimension, and compared against the scores its maintainers publish. No file inside the upstream checkout was modified.

Two techniques did most of the work. First, we read per-item failure records rather than aggregate scores — the benchmark publishes, alongside each score, a record per failed item including the raw model output. Nearly every finding here came from reading those. Second, we built a replay harness that reconstructs a parser's final output byte-identically from stored per-element artifacts and re-scores it with the benchmark's own rule classes, so changes to output formatting are measurable without re-running inference. It reproduces the vendored functions byte-for-byte across 2,041 artifacts and aborts if that check fails.

One control experiment is load-bearing. When we could not reproduce one pipeline's score, the cause could have been our configuration or its environment. We therefore ran a second, unrelated pipeline with its own published scores: it reproduced its published chart score exactly (65.10 against 65.10) and its formatting score within 3.17. A fault in our setup would have moved both. It did not.

3A category the leading pipeline cannot emit

ParseBench scores document parsing over ~2,079 pages across five dimensions using 169,011 deterministic rule checks. We reproduced its leading open-weight entry — a 1.2-billion-parameter model — over the complete corpus.

Three dimensions reproduce; two do not. Investigating grounding, the sub-scores localise the problem: localization — whether predicted regions land in the right place — is the pipeline's strongest sub-metric at 0.869, while classification is its weakest at 0.784. The boxes are right; the labels on them are wrong.

The pipeline converts each raw layout label the model emits into a benchmark category through a 26-key lookup table. Any label absent from that table falls through to Text. Across all 2,078 stored artifacts, the category Section-header is emitted zero times, and the branch of code that would render it is unreachable. Meanwhile the ground truth's second-largest class carries 3,001 rules — 18.4% of all 16,325 grounding rules.

Probing the conversion function directly shows the failure is spelling-sensitive, which is why it had gone unnoticed:

raw label → resulting category

section-header → Section-header  (canonicalisation rescues it)
section_header → Text
paragraph_title → Text
sub_title → Text

One spelling survives; three fall through silently. We cannot yet report the size of this effect, and the reason is itself worth recording: the stored artifacts persist only the mapped category, never the raw label, so which spelling the model emits is unrecoverable from published run outputs. Establishing it requires re-running inference with the conversion instrumented. We have released that instrumentation; the measurement is pending.

4A chart rule that requires bold titles

ParseBench's chart dimension checks whether annotated data points were extracted with correct labels. Its rule accepts a chart's title label only when that title is rendered as bold or as a heading. A parser that extracts every data point correctly but renders the chart title as plain text loses those points.

This is measurable as a side effect: adding bold markup elsewhere in the output, with no change whatever to chart extraction, raised the chart score from 63.69 to 64.95. The extraction did not improve. Only the typography did.

5Three annotated categories with zero weight

The dataset carefully annotates underline, italic and highlight spans. None of them belongs to any scored category — the styling sub-score is assembled from bold, strikethrough, superscript and subscript only. This is visible in the benchmark's own output: a page carrying two underline rules and one strikethrough rule reports that only the strikethrough was counted.

The consequence is wasted effort rather than a wrong score, and it is undiscoverable from the documentation. We spent most of a working day on underline before measuring that its ceiling is exactly zero. Either the annotations should be scored or their status should be stated.

6A coupling that makes the formatting dimension movable

The formatting dimension's bold check accepts two kinds of evidence: an explicit bold marker, or the annotated text appearing on a heading line. The second is reasonable — headings render bold.

But the leading pipeline emits no inline markup at all. Across 23,802 elements from 1,196 documents there is not one bold, underline, strikethrough, superscript or subscript marker. The cause is its prompts: the complete instruction set to the model is five bare strings ("Text Recognition:" and four siblings), with no formatting request anywhere in 3,323 lines. The model is never asked.

Those two facts compose badly. If a pipeline emits no bold markers, 100% of its bold credit must arrive through the heading route — so promoting additional lines to headings raises the bold score without detecting any bold. We verified this: promoting short body-text lines and list items to headings, changing nothing about reading accuracy, moves the overall score by three points, and a single twenty-line output transformation is enough to exceed the published leader.

We report this rather than submit it. It is a property of the scorer, not of any parser. A robust fix is to require an explicit marker when the annotated span is not itself a heading in the ground truth.

7The corpus is majority insurance in one dimension

We characterised the corpus by three independent passes — filenames, all 169,011 ground-truth rule strings, and the text layer of 2,037 PDFs — inspecting every candidate individually. 384 of 2,079 documents (18.5%) are insurance, but they are heavily concentrated.

8Our own results

Having diagnosed the formatting deficit as an emission problem, we repaired what could honestly be called defects: deriving heading levels from geometry the pipeline already had, adding the missing category-map key, and restoring bold on run-in label prefixes that are bold in the source documents. We trained nothing.

9Limitations

  • The reproduction gap is unexplained. We account for the formatting deficit's mechanism but not why our run differs from the published one by 3.71 points overall. Two candidate causes — an optional chart normaliser and output truncation — were tested and eliminated.
  • The grounding effect is unquantified. §3 establishes the mechanism, not its size, because published artifacts discard the raw labels needed to measure it.
  • Uncertainty. The submitted figure carries ±0.2; provider-level transfer error is unmeasured on 157 of 476 documents. Two dimensions were verified invariant under our changes rather than recomputed.
  • The map-key repair contributes exactly zero to any number reported here, for the reason in §3. It ships because the defect is real by inspection, not because it was measured.
  • Insurance sub-scores outside Tables rest on 24–40 pages and are directional; no confidence intervals were computed.
  • One figure is inherited rather than re-measured: the twenty-line transformation in §6 exceeding the leader comes from an earlier measurement pass we did not repeat.

10Reproducibility

Measurement code, the replay harness, saved measurement records for every figure, and the pinned upstream commit are released. The harness enforces two guarantees and aborts if either fails: our re-implementations reproduce the vendored functions byte-for-byte across 2,041 artifacts, and the patched provider reproduces its replay measurement to ten decimal places through the same code path live inference uses. Every figure above is re-derivable without a GPU except the pending §4 measurement, which requires one instrumented inference run.

Correspondence and corrections welcome. We would rather these defects were fixed than cited — three of the four are one-line repairs, and all four affect published numbers that other people are currently comparing.
